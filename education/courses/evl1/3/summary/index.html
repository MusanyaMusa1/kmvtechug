Judicial Notice - All Key Points
  • Judicial notice is the process by which a court accepts certain facts as established without requiring formal proof from the parties
  • It is an exception to the general rule under Section 5 of the Evidence Act Cap. 8 that all facts must be proved through evidence
  • Judicial notice promotes efficiency, recognises judicial expertise, prevents abuse of process, and ensures consistency
  • There are two categories: mandatory judicial notice (court must accept) and discretionary judicial notice (court may accept)
  • Mandatory judicial notice covers: all laws in force in Uganda, the Constitution of Uganda 1995, existence and powers of courts, official seals and signatures, and established national facts
  • Mandatory judicial notice is conclusive - the opposing party cannot produce evidence to contradict mandatorily noticed facts
  • The test for discretionary judicial notice is whether a fact is so notorious or clearly established that formal evidence would be superfluous
  • Discretionary judicial notice can have a localised dimension - what is notorious in one part of Uganda may require proof before a court in another part
  • Good practice requires parties to be heard before a court takes discretionary judicial notice
  • Discretionarily noticed facts can in principle be challenged - object before the court takes notice, not after
  • When citing statutes, always use the correct Cap number under the 2024 Revised Edition - judicial notice covers what the law actually is, not an incorrect citation
  • Judicial notice does NOT cover a judge's personal knowledge of specific disputed facts in the case - that is grounds for disqualification
  • Uganda Electricity Board v G.W. Musoke, CA No. 30 of 1993 - courts work from what is properly established and do not assume genuinely disputed facts
  • In examinations: always identify whether notice is mandatory or discretionary and explain the practical consequences
