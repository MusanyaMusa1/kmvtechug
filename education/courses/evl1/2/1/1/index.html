Video Summary

This video introduces relevancy using a Kampala poisoning scenario. It explains why only some of the five pieces of information about the accused should be heard by the court, defines relevancy as the logical connection between a fact and the matter in issue, explains the probative value test, and introduces the foundational provision of Section 5 of the Evidence Act Cap. 8.

Opening Scenario

A woman is on trial in Kampala for poisoning her husband. The prosecution wants to introduce the following pieces of information:

The Five Pieces of Information

1. The accused purchased rat poison from a shop in Wandegeya three days before her husband died.

2. The accused had taken out a large life insurance policy on her husband six months earlier.

3. The accused was seen arguing loudly with her husband the evening before his death.

4. The accused once failed an examination in secondary school fifteen years ago.

5. The accused's neighbour thinks she is generally a dishonest person.

Which of these should the court hear? Which ones actually help the court decide whether the accused poisoned her husband? Which ones, while perhaps interesting, have nothing reliable to tell the court about what happened? This is the problem of relevancy.

Definition of Relevancy

Relevancy is the logical connection between a fact and the matter in issue before the court. A fact is relevant if, by itself or in combination with other facts, it makes the existence of a fact in issue more probable or less probable than it would be without that fact.

The Evidence Act Cap. 8 does not define relevancy in a single provision. Instead, it identifies categories of relevant facts across Sections 5 to 16. The foundational provision is Section 5 which provides that evidence may be given of facts in issue and of such other facts as are declared relevant by the Act.

Facts in Issue vs Relevant Facts

Facts in issue are the specific facts a party must prove or disprove to succeed in their case. They arise directly from the substantive law. In this murder case under the Penal Code Act Cap. 128, the facts in issue include whether the accused caused the death, whether the death was unlawful, and whether the accused had the requisite intention.

Relevant facts are facts that are not themselves in issue but which make the facts in issue more or less probable. Returning to the scenario: the purchase of rat poison is a relevant fact. It is not itself a fact in issue but it makes it more probable that the accused had access to poison. The failed secondary school examination, on the other hand, has no logical connection to whether she poisoned someone. It is irrelevant.

The Test for Relevancy

The test for whether a fact is relevant is a logical one. Ask: does this fact, if true, make it more or less probable that something that is in issue actually occurred? This is called the probative value test. A fact has probative value if it proves or tends to prove something that matters to the case.

If the answer is yes: the fact is relevant. If the answer is no: the fact is irrelevant and should not be admitted.

Why Relevancy Matters

Relevancy is the first gate that all evidence must pass. Before a court asks whether evidence is admissible, it must first ask whether that evidence is relevant. Evidence that is not relevant cannot be admitted, regardless of how it was obtained or how reliable it might be.

The relevancy rule also serves the efficiency objective of evidence law. If courts heard every piece of information a party wanted to introduce regardless of its connection to the case, proceedings would become unmanageable. It also protects fairness by filtering out material like the failed examination which risks decisions being influenced by prejudice rather than proof.

Key Authority

Key Authorities

Evidence Act Cap. 8, Section 5 - Foundational provision: evidence may be given of facts in issue and facts declared relevant.

Evidence Act Cap. 8, Sections 6 to 16 - Specific categories of relevant facts.

Makin v Attorney General of New South Wales [1894] AC 57 (Privy Council) - Similar facts may be relevant where they show a system or pattern connected to the matter in issue. Cited as persuasive authority in Ugandan courts.

Key Points - Page 2.1.1
  • Relevancy is the logical connection between a fact and the matter in issue before the court
  • A fact is relevant if it makes a fact in issue more or less probable - this is the probative value test
  • Facts in issue are what must be proved to succeed - they arise from the applicable substantive law
  • Relevant facts are not themselves in issue but have a logical connection to facts in issue
  • Relevancy is the first gate - evidence that is not relevant cannot be admitted at all
  • The primary authority is the Evidence Act Cap. 8, Sections 5 to 16
