Video Summary

This video works through the specific statutory framework of relevancy under the Evidence Act Cap. 8 from Section 5 to Section 16. It explains how the Act declares specific categories of facts relevant and why this statutory approach differs from the English common law approach. Key sections covered include Section 6 (res gestae), Section 7 (cause and effect), Section 8 (motive and preparation), Sections 10 and 11, and Sections 14 to 16 on similar fact evidence. The Makin case is discussed as persuasive Commonwealth authority.

The Statutory Framework

The Evidence Act Cap. 8 does not leave relevancy to general principles alone. It specifically identifies categories of facts that are relevant in Sections 5 to 16. This statutory approach - declaring facts relevant rather than leaving it entirely to judicial discretion - is one of the features Uganda inherited from the Indian Evidence Act 1872.

This is different from the English common law approach which historically gave judges more discretion in determining relevancy. In Uganda, if a fact falls within one of the categories declared relevant by the Evidence Act Cap. 8, it is relevant as a matter of law, not merely as a matter of judicial opinion.

Section 5 - The Foundational Provision

Section 5 of the Evidence Act Cap. 8 is the gateway provision. It provides that evidence may be given of facts in issue and of such other facts as are declared relevant by the Act. Two important points arise: first, facts in issue are always provable. Second, for any other fact to be provable, it must be declared relevant somewhere in the Act. This gives the relevancy provisions enormous importance.

Sections 6 to 11 - Key Relevancy Categories

Section 6 - Facts forming part of the same transaction (res gestae). Facts so connected with a fact in issue as to form part of the same transaction are relevant. In a Kampala robbery case, the words shouted by the accused during the robbery, the actions of bystanders, and the immediate chase that followed all form part of the same transaction as the robbery itself.

Section 7 - Facts which are the occasion, cause or effect of facts in issue. In a road accident case in Jinja, the condition of the road surface, the weather at the time, and the speed of the vehicle are all facts that may be the cause or occasion of the accident which is the fact in issue.

Section 8 - Motive, preparation, and previous or subsequent conduct. In the poisoning scenario from Page 1, the accused's purchase of rat poison shows preparation. Her argument with the deceased the evening before shows potential motive. Both are relevant under Section 8.

Section 9 - Facts necessary to explain or introduce relevant facts. This section ensures the court receives enough context to properly understand and evaluate the relevant facts put before it.

Section 10 - Things said or done by a conspirator in reference to a common design. Where there is reasonable ground to believe two or more persons have conspired together, anything said, done, or written by any one of them in reference to their common intention after it was formed is relevant as against each of those persons.

Section 11 - Residual relevancy. Facts not otherwise relevant are relevant if they are inconsistent with any fact in issue or relevant fact, or if they make the existence or non-existence of any fact in issue highly probable or improbable. This is the flexibility provision that catches highly probative facts not falling neatly within earlier categories.

Sections 14 to 16 - State of Mind and Similar Facts

Section 14 deals with facts showing the existence of a state of mind or body. Where the existence of a particular intention, knowledge, good faith, negligence, or ill-will is in issue, facts showing the existence of that state of mind are relevant.

Section 15 deals with whether an act was accidental or intentional. The fact that an act formed part of a series of similar occurrences in which the same person was involved is relevant. This is the statutory basis for similar fact evidence in Uganda.

Persuasive Commonwealth Authority

Makin v Attorney General of New South Wales [1894] AC 57 (Privy Council) - Evidence of similar acts may be admissible where they show a system or plan connected to the matter in issue, but may NOT be admitted merely to show the accused is a person of bad character. This principle is consistent with Section 15 and is regularly cited as persuasive authority in Ugandan courts.

The Practical Workflow

In Ugandan courts the practical workflow is as follows: when a party seeks to introduce evidence, the first question is whether the fact it tends to prove is relevant under the Evidence Act Cap. 8. If the fact is relevant, the second question is whether the evidence offered to prove it is admissible under the Act's admissibility rules, which we study in Lesson 2.
