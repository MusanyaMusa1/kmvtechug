Consequences of Inadmissible Evidence

Text~8 min reading

What Happens When Evidence is Excluded?

When a court rules that evidence is inadmissible, that evidence is removed from consideration entirely. The court proceeds to decide the case as though that evidence was never presented. This has significant practical consequences for both parties.

Consequences for the Party Who Offered the Evidence

The party who offered the inadmissible evidence loses the benefit of it entirely. In a criminal case, if the prosecution's main evidence - such as a confession - is excluded on admissibility grounds, the prosecution must prove its case using whatever other evidence remains. If no other sufficient evidence exists, the accused may be acquitted not because they are innocent but because the available evidence cannot legally be used.

This reflects a deliberate policy choice in Ugandan law. The protection of constitutional rights and the integrity of the justice system is considered more important than securing a conviction at all costs. A justice system that admits illegally obtained evidence creates incentives for police and prosecutors to break the law in the course of investigation.

Consequences for the Opposing Party

The opposing party benefits from exclusion, but only to the extent that the remaining evidence is insufficient to prove the case against them. Exclusion of evidence does not automatically mean the opposing party wins. The court evaluates all remaining admissible evidence to determine whether the required standard of proof is met.

Admissibility vs Weight - A Critical Distinction

Admissibility and weight are two different things. Admissibility determines whether evidence gets into the court at all. Weight determines how much significance the court gives to evidence that has been admitted.

A piece of evidence may be admissible but carry very little weight - for example, the uncorroborated testimony of a single witness who was far from the scene. Another piece of evidence may be admissible and carry great weight - such as a clear CCTV recording of the accused committing the offence.

An advocate who confuses admissibility with weight makes a fundamental error. Objecting to admissibility when the evidence is clearly admissible wastes the court's time. The right strategy in such situations is to accept admission but challenge the weight through cross-examination and submissions.

Wrongly Admitted Evidence - Grounds for Appeal

If a trial court wrongly admits evidence that should have been excluded, this is an error of adjectival law that can be raised as a ground of appeal. The appellate court will examine whether the error affected the outcome of the case.

Not every error in admitting evidence leads to a successful appeal. If the remaining admissible evidence was sufficient to support the decision even without the wrongly admitted evidence, the appellate court may uphold the decision despite the error. This is sometimes called the proviso - the principle that a conviction will not be overturned merely because of an error that did not materially affect the outcome.

Key Points - Page 2.2.3
  • When evidence is excluded as inadmissible, it is removed entirely from the court's consideration
  • A party whose key evidence is excluded must rely on remaining admissible evidence - if insufficient, they may lose
  • Exclusion does not automatically mean the opposing party wins - the remaining evidence is still evaluated
  • Admissibility and weight are different - admissibility is about getting evidence before the court, weight is about how much significance the court gives it
  • Wrongly admitting inadmissible evidence is an error of adjectival law that can be a ground of appeal
  • The proviso: not every admissibility error leads to a successful appeal if the remaining evidence was sufficient
