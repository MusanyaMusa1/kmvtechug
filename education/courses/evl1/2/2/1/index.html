Video Summary

This video introduces admissibility as the second gate evidence must pass after relevancy. It opens with a Kampala murder suspect who confesses after being beaten at a police station, uses this scenario to show how relevant evidence can still be excluded, defines admissibility, explains the two-gate system, and covers the five common grounds for excluding relevant evidence including torture-obtained confessions, hearsay, privilege, opinion evidence, and character evidence.

Opening Scenario

A murder suspect in Uganda is arrested. During interrogation at the police station, police officers beat him severely until he confesses to the killing. The confession is written down and signed. When the case reaches the High Court, the prosecution wants to use this confession as evidence.

Is the confession relevant? Yes - it directly addresses whether the accused committed the murder. It makes it more probable that he did. But should the court admit it? This is where admissibility comes in. Even though the confession is relevant, the law may still exclude it. A confession obtained through torture violates Article 24 of the Constitution of Uganda 1995. The court must decide not only whether the evidence is relevant but whether it is admissible.

Definition of Admissibility

Admissibility is the quality of evidence that makes it legally receivable by a court. Evidence is admissible if it satisfies all the legal requirements that allow it to be presented to and considered by the court.

Not all relevant evidence is admissible. Some evidence, though logically relevant, is excluded because it was obtained unlawfully, because it falls within a category that the law excludes on policy grounds, or because it fails to meet specific procedural requirements. The Evidence Act Cap. 8 governs admissibility across several provisions dealing with different types of evidence.

Admissibility as the Second Gate

Think of the process of getting evidence before a court as passing through two gates in sequence.

The first gate is relevancy. Evidence that is not relevant is stopped here and goes no further. Nothing that is irrelevant can ever be admitted.

The second gate is admissibility. Evidence that passes the relevancy test must then satisfy all legal requirements for admissibility. If it fails at this gate - because it was illegally obtained, because it is hearsay outside the permitted exceptions, because a confession was not properly taken - it is excluded even though it was relevant.

Only evidence that passes both gates reaches the court and can be used in reaching a decision.

Common Grounds for Excluding Relevant Evidence

Illegally obtained evidence. Confessions obtained through torture, coercion, or threats are inadmissible. Article 24 of the Constitution of Uganda 1995 prohibits torture absolutely. A confession obtained in violation of this provision will be excluded regardless of how relevant it is.

Hearsay. A statement made outside court by a person who is not testifying cannot generally be admitted as evidence of the truth of its contents. The Evidence Act Cap. 8 provides specific exceptions in Section 30, including dying declarations, statements against interest, and statements made in the course of business. Outside these exceptions, hearsay is inadmissible even if relevant.

Privilege. Legal professional privilege protects communications between a lawyer and their client. Spousal privilege protects certain communications between married partners. These privileged communications cannot be admitted even if highly relevant.

Opinion evidence. Witnesses generally give evidence of facts they personally observed, not their opinions. The exception is expert witnesses with specialised knowledge permitted to give opinion evidence within their expertise. A neighbour's opinion on whether the accused is generally dishonest is generally inadmissible.

Character evidence. Evidence of a person's general bad character is generally inadmissible to prove they committed the act in question. There are specific exceptions under the Evidence Act Cap. 8, particularly where the accused puts their own character in issue.

Key Ugandan Cases

Key Cases on Admissibility

Stanbic Bank Uganda Ltd v Uganda Crocs Ltd, Civil Appeal No. 4 of 2004 - The Court of Appeal confirmed that documents must be properly authenticated before they can be admitted. Relevancy alone is not sufficient.

Akbar Hussein Godi v Uganda, Criminal Appeal No. 3 of 2013 - The Supreme Court considered admissibility of identification evidence and the requirements that must be met before such evidence can properly be placed before the court and acted upon.

Key Points - Page 2.2.1
  • Admissibility is the quality that makes evidence legally receivable by a court
  • Not all relevant evidence is admissible - relevancy is necessary but not sufficient
  • Admissibility is the second gate - evidence must pass both relevancy and admissibility to reach the court
  • Common grounds for excluding relevant evidence include illegal obtaining, hearsay, privilege, improper opinion, and character evidence
  • The Evidence Act Cap. 8 and the Constitution of Uganda 1995 both govern admissibility
