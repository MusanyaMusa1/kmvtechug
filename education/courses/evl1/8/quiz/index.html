Section A - Multiple Choice (12 questions)
Question 1 of 25
What is the definition of corroboration from R v Baskerville [1916] 2 KB 658?
A
Any evidence consistent with the complainant's account
B
Independent testimony affecting the accused by connecting him with the crime and confirming in a material particular
C
Evidence from more than one witness
D
A signed confession supporting eyewitness testimony
R v Baskerville defines corroboration as independent testimony affecting the accused by connecting him with the crime - confirming in a material particular not only that the crime was committed but that the accused committed it.
Not correct. Baskerville: independent source, implicates the accused, material particular. The correct answer is B.

Section B - True or False (7 questions)
Question 13 of 25
A witness can corroborate their own testimony by making an earlier consistent statement to police.
False. A witness cannot corroborate themselves. An earlier consistent statement by the same witness comes from the same source as the testimony being corroborated. It does not satisfy the independence requirement.
False is correct. A witness cannot corroborate themselves.
Question 14 of 25
R v Baskerville [1916] 2 KB 658 is binding authority on Ugandan courts.
False. R v Baskerville is persuasive Commonwealth authority in Uganda - not binding. Ugandan courts regularly apply it because it provides a reliable definition consistent with the common law heritage of Uganda's Evidence Act Cap. 8.
False is correct. R v Baskerville is persuasive not binding.
Question 15 of 25
Under the modern Ugandan position, a court can convict in a sexual offence case on the uncorroborated testimony of the complainant alone if satisfied of its reliability.
True. The modern position has modified the historical mandatory corroboration requirement in sexual offence cases. A court genuinely satisfied of the reliability of the complainant's testimony after careful consideration can convict on that testimony alone.
True is correct. The modern position allows conviction on sole reliable testimony.
Question 16 of 25
One accomplice can corroborate another accomplice's testimony because their accounts come from two separate people.
False. The number of people making the same claim is not sufficient for Baskerville corroboration. Both accomplices are in the same unreliable category - tainted evidence requiring independent corroboration from outside their category.
False is correct. One accomplice cannot corroborate another.
Question 17 of 25
Ntambala v Uganda CA No. 34 of 2015 held that trial courts must genuinely engage with corroboration warnings rather than merely recite a formula.
True. Ntambala v Uganda required genuine engagement with the corroboration question - actually considering the specific dangers and whether corroboration exists. A formulaic warning without genuine analysis is insufficient.
True is correct. Ntambala v Uganda required genuine engagement.
Question 18 of 25
Medical evidence showing injuries consistent with the alleged attack always amounts to Baskerville corroboration.
False. Medical evidence of injuries does not automatically satisfy Baskerville. For it to corroborate, it must implicate the accused - connect the specific accused to causing the injuries. If it only confirms injuries occurred without connecting the accused, the implication requirement is not satisfied.
False is correct. Medical evidence must also implicate the accused to satisfy Baskerville.
