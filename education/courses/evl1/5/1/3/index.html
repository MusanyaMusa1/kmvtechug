The Evidential Burden

Text~9 min reading

What is the Evidential Burden?

The evidential burden - also called the burden of adducing evidence or the tactical burden - is the obligation on a party to raise sufficient evidence on a particular issue to make that issue a live question before the court. It differs from the legal burden in two critical ways: it can shift between parties during the course of proceedings, and failure to discharge it does not necessarily mean losing the case - it means the issue is not put before the court for consideration.

How the Evidential Burden Works in Practice

At the start of criminal proceedings, the prosecution bears both the legal burden and the initial evidential burden - it must adduce evidence on every element of the offence. Once the prosecution has called sufficient evidence to establish a prima facie case on every element, the evidential burden on those elements is discharged and the case is put to the accused.

If the accused then raises a specific defence - alibi, self-defence, provocation, duress - the accused bears the evidential burden of adducing sufficient evidence to make that defence a live issue. The accused does not need to prove the defence on the balance of probabilities - they only need to raise sufficient evidence to put the defence before the court. Once sufficient evidence is raised, the prosecution must deal with it.

Key Authority on Prima Facie Case

Francis Xavier Kayemba v Uganda [1983] HCB 330 - A prima facie case requires evidence on which a reasonable tribunal could convict if the defence offers no explanation. This standard marks the threshold at which the prosecution discharges its initial evidential burden and the case is put to the accused.

The No-Case Submission

The evidential burden is directly relevant to the no-case submission - a procedural device in criminal trials where the defence submits at the close of the prosecution case that the prosecution has not adduced sufficient evidence to establish a prima facie case on any element of the offence and that the accused should be acquitted without being called on to answer.

If the court upholds the no-case submission it means the prosecution has failed to discharge its initial evidential burden on at least one element of the offence. The accused is acquitted not because they have proved innocence but because the prosecution has not raised sufficient evidence to justify requiring the accused to respond.

Evidential Burden vs Legal Burden - The Key Distinction

The legal burden determines who must ultimately win on an issue. The evidential burden determines who must raise the issue at a particular point. These can be on the same party or different parties at different stages of proceedings.

In a murder trial: the prosecution bears the legal burden throughout. After calling sufficient evidence, the prosecution discharges the initial evidential burden. If the accused raises alibi, the accused discharges the evidential burden on alibi. The prosecution then bears the evidential burden of responding to the alibi. But the legal burden of disproving the alibi never shifted - it was always on the prosecution.

Key Points - Page 5.1.3
  • The evidential burden is the obligation to raise sufficient evidence to put an issue before the court - it can shift during proceedings
  • The prosecution bears the initial evidential burden - must establish a prima facie case on every element
  • Prima facie case standard: evidence on which a reasonable tribunal could convict if the defence offers no explanation - Francis Xavier Kayemba v Uganda [1983] HCB 330
  • A no-case submission challenges whether the prosecution has discharged its evidential burden at close of its case
  • When the accused raises a specific defence, the accused bears the evidential burden of raising it sufficiently
  • The legal burden of disproving a defence never shifts from the prosecution in criminal cases
