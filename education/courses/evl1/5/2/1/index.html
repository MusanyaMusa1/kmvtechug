When Does the Burden Shift?

Video~12 minEvidence Act Cap. 8

PDF notes downloadable below.

Video Summary

This video clarifies that when lawyers say the burden shifts they almost always mean the evidential burden not the legal burden. It explains the shifting evidential burden using the spotlight metaphor, traces the movement of the evidential burden through a complete criminal trial from prosecution opening through to alibi evidence, and explains how burden shifting works differently in civil proceedings.

The Concept of Shifting

When lawyers and judges talk about the burden shifting, they almost always mean the evidential burden - not the legal burden. This is a source of enormous confusion for students and must be understood clearly.

The legal burden in criminal cases does not shift. The prosecution bears it from the opening of the case to the verdict. Nothing the defence does shifts this legal burden onto the accused.

The evidential burden, however, moves back and forth. Think of it as a spotlight. At any given point in the trial, the spotlight of evidential obligation falls on one party - they must adduce sufficient evidence to keep the relevant issue alive. When they do so, the spotlight moves to the other party.

How the Evidential Burden Shifts in a Criminal Trial

At the start of a criminal trial, both the legal burden and the initial evidential burden are on the prosecution. The prosecution begins by calling its evidence - witnesses, documents, physical exhibits.

Once the prosecution closes its case, the court assesses whether the prosecution has established a prima facie case using the standard from Francis Xavier Kayemba v Uganda [1983] HCB 330 - evidence on which a reasonable tribunal could convict if the defence offers no explanation.

If the prosecution has not established a prima facie case, the defence may submit no case to answer. If the court upholds this, the accused is acquitted - the prosecution has failed to discharge even the initial evidential burden.

If the prosecution has established a prima facie case, the accused is called upon to answer. The evidential burden effectively shifts - the accused must decide whether to give evidence, call witnesses, or rely on the weaknesses in the prosecution case.

Specific Defences and the Shifting Evidential Burden

When the accused raises a specific defence - self-defence, provocation, alibi, mistake of fact, duress - the accused bears the evidential burden of raising that defence sufficiently. Merely asserting the defence without evidence to support it is insufficient.

Once the accused discharges the evidential burden on the defence - by raising sufficient evidence to make it a genuine issue - the evidential burden shifts back to the prosecution. The prosecution must adduce evidence or make submissions to counter the defence.

The legal burden of disproving the defence remains on the prosecution throughout. The prosecution is not absolved of its legal burden merely because the accused bore the evidential burden of raising the defence initially.

Shifting in Civil Proceedings

In civil proceedings, the shifting of burdens is more flexible. As each party makes assertions, they bear the burden on those assertions. In a contract dispute: the plaintiff first bears the burden of proving the contract and the breach. If the defendant asserts misrepresentation, the defendant bears the burden of proving the misrepresentation.

The practical consequence is that in civil proceedings, burden analysis must be done issue by issue rather than for the case as a whole. Each party may bear burdens on different issues and the party who fails to discharge their burden on any particular issue loses on that issue.

Key Points - Page 5.2.1
  • When lawyers say the burden shifts in a criminal trial, they mean the evidential burden - the legal burden does not shift
  • The evidential burden works like a spotlight - moving between parties as each responds to what the other has raised
  • In criminal trials: prosecution bears initial evidential burden, discharges it by establishing prima facie case, accused then responds
  • On specific defences: accused bears evidential burden of raising them sufficiently, prosecution must then respond
  • The legal burden of disproving a defence never shifts from the prosecution in criminal cases
  • In civil cases: burden analysis is issue by issue - each party bears the burden on what they assert
