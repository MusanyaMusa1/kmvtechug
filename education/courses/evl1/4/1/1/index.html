Video Summary

This video introduces estoppel using a Kampala landlord scenario where a tenant relies on a letter confirming rent and signs a business contract. It defines estoppel under Section 114 of the Evidence Act Cap. 8, explains the three elements of representation, reliance, and detriment, explains why estoppel is an evidence rule, and discusses the tension between estoppel and the search for truth.

Opening Scenario

A landlord in Kampala writes a letter to his tenant confirming that the monthly rent is UGX 300,000 and will remain so for the next two years. The tenant, relying on this letter, signs a business contract with a supplier that assumes rental costs will remain at UGX 300,000. Six months later the landlord demands UGX 600,000 and threatens eviction.

The landlord may argue the letter was not a formal contract. He may even be correct as a matter of pure contract law. But in a Ugandan court, the landlord faces a powerful obstacle: estoppel. Having made a clear representation that the rent would remain at UGX 300,000, and having watched the tenant act on that representation to his detriment, the landlord may be estopped from asserting that the rent is now UGX 600,000.

Definition of Estoppel

Estoppel is a rule of evidence that prevents a person from asserting or denying a fact when that person has previously made a representation, taken a position, or caused another to believe something to be true, and that other person has acted on that belief to their detriment.

The word estoppel comes from the old French estoupail meaning a stopper or plug. The doctrine plugs the mouth of a person who would otherwise be allowed to say something inconsistent with what they have previously represented.

Section 114, Evidence Act Cap. 8

When one person has by their declaration, act, or omission intentionally caused or permitted another person to believe a thing to be true and to act upon such belief, neither that person nor their representative in interest shall be allowed to deny the truth of that thing in any suit or proceeding between that person and such other person or their representative in interest.

The Three Elements of Estoppel

For estoppel to operate under Section 114, three elements must all be established.

First - a representation or conduct. There must be a clear representation by the party to be estopped. This can be a statement, an act, or an omission. Silence can amount to a representation where there was a duty to speak. The representation must be clear and unambiguous.

Second - reliance. The other party must have actually relied on the representation. They must have believed it to be true and acted on that belief. If the other party knew the representation was false, or did not act on it at all, estoppel cannot arise.

Third - detriment. The relying party must have suffered detriment as a result of their reliance. They must be worse off because they relied on the representation. If no detriment resulted, the doctrine has nothing to protect against.

All three elements must be present. If any one is missing, estoppel will not arise even if the other two are clearly established.

Why Estoppel is an Evidence Rule

Students sometimes ask why estoppel appears in the Evidence Act Cap. 8 rather than in a contract statute. The answer lies in what estoppel actually does. Estoppel is fundamentally a rule about what a party will and will not be permitted to assert before a court. It prevents a party from introducing evidence or relying on an argument that contradicts a position they have previously taken. This is an evidence rule because it regulates what can and cannot be said and proved before the court.

Estoppel does not create or destroy rights in themselves. The landlord still technically owns the property. What estoppel does is prevent him from asserting, before the court, that the rent is UGX 600,000 when he has previously represented it would be UGX 300,000 and the tenant has relied on that.

Estoppel and the Search for Truth

Estoppel creates an interesting tension with the truth-seeking objective of evidence law. By preventing a party from asserting what might actually be the strict legal truth, estoppel privileges consistency and the protection of reasonable reliance over strict legal accuracy. This reflects an important value judgment: a person should not be permitted to blow hot and cold - to make a representation that another relies on and then contradict it when convenient.

Key Authority

Stanbic Bank Uganda Ltd v Uganda Crocs Ltd, Civil Appeal No. 4 of 2004 - The Court of Appeal of Uganda examined how representations made by one party affected the rights of the other and the extent to which a party could be held to positions previously taken. Illustrates the Ugandan courts' approach to representations and their consequences.

Key Points - Page 4.1.1
  • Estoppel prevents a person from asserting or denying a fact inconsistent with a previous representation when another has relied on it to their detriment
  • The governing provision is Section 114 of the Evidence Act Cap. 8
  • Three elements must all be established: representation or conduct, actual reliance, and detriment suffered
  • Estoppel is an evidence rule because it regulates what a party can assert before a court - not a rule creating or destroying substantive rights
  • Estoppel reflects the principle that a person must not blow hot and cold - making a representation and contradicting it when convenient
