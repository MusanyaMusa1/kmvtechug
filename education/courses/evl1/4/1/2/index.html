Estoppel by Record

Text~9 min reading

What is Estoppel by Record?

Estoppel by record - also known as estoppel per rem judicatam or res judicata - operates where a court has already made a final judgment on a matter and prevents the same parties from relitigating that same matter in subsequent proceedings. The principle is straightforward: a matter that has been finally decided by a court of competent jurisdiction should not be reopened. There must be finality in litigation. If parties could simply return to court and relitigate matters already decided, court proceedings would have no end and judgments would have no authority.

Two Forms of Estoppel by Record

Cause of action estoppel prevents a party from bringing proceedings on a cause of action that has already been finally determined between the same parties by a court of competent jurisdiction. If a Ugandan court has finally decided that Party A owes Party B UGX 5 million in a contract dispute, Party B cannot bring another action claiming the same UGX 5 million under the same contract. The cause of action is spent.

Issue estoppel prevents a party from relitigating a specific issue of fact or law that was necessarily decided as part of an earlier case between the same parties. Even if the cause of action in the second case is different, if a specific issue was already finally determined in the first case, that issue cannot be reopened. If an earlier case conclusively determined that a particular signature was genuine, neither party can argue in later proceedings between them that the signature was forged.

Requirements for Estoppel by Record

For estoppel by record to apply in Ugandan courts, four requirements must all be satisfied.

The earlier decision must have been made by a court of competent jurisdiction. A decision by a court that lacked jurisdiction does not create estoppel by record.

The decision must have been final. Interlocutory orders and procedural decisions made during proceedings do not create estoppel by record - only final decisions on the merits.

The parties in the second proceeding must be the same as those in the first, or must be claiming through them. Estoppel by record does not bind strangers to the earlier litigation.

The issue or cause of action must be the same as that decided in the earlier proceedings.

What Must Have Been Decided

In practice, advocates checking whether issue estoppel applies must examine the earlier judgment carefully to determine what was actually decided - not merely what was argued or discussed in passing. Only what was necessarily decided for the judgment to be reached creates issue estoppel. Observations made obiter dictum - things the court said but which were not necessary for its decision - do not create issue estoppel.

The Judicature Act Cap. 16 establishes the court hierarchy in Uganda. Decisions of superior courts on matters within their jurisdiction are binding and create estoppel against parties to those proceedings in all subsequent litigation on the same matter.

Key Points - Page 4.1.2
  • Estoppel by record prevents parties from relitigating matters already finally decided by a court of competent jurisdiction
  • It operates in two forms: cause of action estoppel (same cause of action) and issue estoppel (same specific issue, even in a different cause of action)
  • Four requirements: competent court, final decision, same parties, same issue or cause of action
  • Only what was necessarily decided for the judgment creates issue estoppel - not every point discussed in the judgment
  • The doctrine promotes finality in litigation - without it court judgments would have no effective authority
  • The Judicature Act Cap. 16 governs the court hierarchy within which estoppel by record operates
