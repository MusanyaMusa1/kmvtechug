Video Summary

This video covers estoppel by conduct - the broadest and most commonly litigated form - using Ugandan land dispute examples and the Owino Market stall scenario. It explains how conduct, silence, and words can create estoppel, the limits of the doctrine, and the critical shield not sword principle.

What is Estoppel by Conduct?

Estoppel by conduct - sometimes called estoppel in pais - is the form of estoppel that arises from the acts, statements, or omissions of a party outside formal court proceedings or formal documents. It is the form of estoppel most directly addressed by Section 114 of the Evidence Act Cap. 8.

Unlike estoppel by record which arises from a court judgment, and estoppel by deed which arises from a formal document, estoppel by conduct can arise from any sufficiently clear representation made by words, actions, or even silence where there was a duty to speak. It is the broadest and most flexible form and appears most frequently in Ugandan civil litigation.

How Estoppel by Conduct Arises

Estoppel by conduct arises when a person makes a representation of fact - not a promise or opinion, but a representation of existing fact - and another person relies on that representation and suffers detriment as a result.

The representation can be made expressly in words: "I confirm the debt has been paid." It can be made by conduct: a creditor who accepts reduced payments over many months without objection may represent by their conduct that they have agreed to the reduced amount. It can even arise from silence where there was a legal duty to correct a false impression: a party who stands by and watches another act on a false belief that the silent party could have corrected may be estopped from later denying the truth of that belief.

Key East African Authority

Kimani v Gikanga (1965) EA 735 - The East African Court of Appeal considered how a party's conduct over time can create an estoppel preventing them from later asserting rights inconsistent with the position their conduct had represented. Regularly cited as persuasive authority in Ugandan courts.

Estoppel by Conduct in Ugandan Land Cases

Estoppel by conduct arises with great frequency in Ugandan land disputes. Consider a situation where a landowner in Mukono allows a person to cultivate and develop land for many years, accepts payments described as "appreciation" rather than rent, never objects to developments, and participates in community ceremonies treating the cultivator as having rights. If the landowner later seeks to evict the cultivator, a Ugandan court may find that the landowner is estopped by conduct from denying the cultivator's rights.

These cases illustrate that estoppel by conduct can have profound practical consequences in Uganda where many land arrangements are informal and long-standing conduct rather than formal documentation defines the relationship between parties.

What Estoppel by Conduct Does NOT Cover

Estoppel by conduct has important limits. First, it cannot be used to override a clear provision of statute. If the law requires a transaction to be in writing to be enforceable, estoppel by conduct cannot substitute for that requirement. Second, it requires a representation of existing fact not a promise about future conduct - that is where promissory estoppel comes in, covered in Page 2. Third, estoppel by conduct cannot create a cause of action where none otherwise exists.

The Shield Not Sword Principle

One of the most examined principles in Ugandan law examinations relating to estoppel is the rule that estoppel is a shield not a sword. This means a party can use estoppel defensively to prevent the other party from asserting something inconsistent with a previous representation, but cannot use it offensively as a cause of action in itself.

In the Kampala landlord scenario from Lesson 1, the tenant uses estoppel as a shield: when the landlord sues for UGX 600,000 rent, the tenant raises estoppel as a defence. The tenant is not using estoppel to create a new right - the tenant is using it to prevent the landlord from asserting an inconsistent position. This is the classic and proper use of estoppel.

Key Points - Page 4.2.1
  • Estoppel by conduct is the broadest form - arising from acts, statements, or omissions outside formal proceedings or documents
  • Governed by Section 114 of the Evidence Act Cap. 8
  • Can arise from express words, conduct over time, or silence where there was a duty to speak
  • Arises frequently in Ugandan land disputes where informal long-standing arrangements create representations
  • Limits: cannot override statute, requires representation of existing fact not mere promise, cannot create a cause of action
  • Estoppel is a shield not a sword - used defensively to prevent inconsistent assertions, not offensively as a claim
  • Key persuasive authority: Kimani v Gikanga (1965) EA 735
