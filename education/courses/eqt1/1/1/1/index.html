Origin of Equity in England and the Earl of Oxford's Case

Video Summary

This page introduces the historical origin of Equity. The common law of England developed as a rigid system of rules that sometimes produced injustice. People began petitioning the King for fairness according to conscience. This power was exercised by the Lord Chancellor through the Court of Chancery, giving birth to Equity. The conflict between common law and Equity was settled in the Earl of Oxford's Case (1615). King James I ruled that where common law and Equity conflict, Equity prevails. This principle remains fundamental in Uganda today.

Why Equity Developed

The common law of England developed as a rigid system of rules. Over time these strict rules sometimes produced unfair results. People who felt the common law had treated them unjustly began to petition the King directly, asking him to do justice according to conscience.

The King delegated this special power to his most important official, the Lord Chancellor. Gradually the Lord Chancellor's court became known as the Court of Chancery. The body of principles developed by the Court of Chancery is what we call Equity.

Key principle
Equity was not created to destroy the common law. It was created to soften the harshness of the common law and to prevent injustice where the strict rules failed.

The Earl of Oxford's Case (1615)

By the early 1600s tension grew between the common law courts and the Court of Chancery. This conflict reached its peak in the famous case of the Earl of Oxford's Case in 1615.

Magdalene College had granted a long lease of land. Later the college tried to recover the land, relying on a strict statute. At common law the college appeared to be correct. However, the Earl of Oxford had spent a great deal of money improving the land. The Court of Chancery held that it would be against conscience for the college to take back the land after the Earl had improved it.

Lord Chancellor Ellesmere
"The cause why there is a Chancery is, for that men's actions are so diverse and infinite, that it is impossible to make any general law which may aptly meet with every particular act, and not fail in some circumstances."

The dispute was taken to King James I. The King decided that where common law and Equity conflict, Equity shall prevail.

Key Points

  • Equity developed to soften the harshness of the common law.
  • The Court of Chancery administered Equity.
  • In the Earl of Oxford's Case (1615), it was decided that Equity prevails over common law in case of conflict.
  • This principle is the foundation of the modern relationship between law and Equity.
