Certainty of Objects

Video Summary

Fixed trusts require a complete list of beneficiaries. Discretionary trusts use the any-given-postulant test from McPhail v Doulton. Conceptual uncertainty defeats both types.

Fixed Trusts

In a fixed trust the beneficiaries and their shares are predetermined. The classic test is the “complete list” test: it must be possible to draw up a complete list of all the beneficiaries. If that is impossible, the trust fails for uncertainty of objects.

Discretionary Trusts

In a discretionary trust the trustees have power to select who among a class will benefit and in what shares. The modern test, established in McPhail v Doulton (also known as Re Baden), is the “any given postulant” test: it must be possible to say with certainty of any given individual whether that person is or is not a member of the class.

Important distinction
Conceptual uncertainty (where the description itself is unclear) will defeat both fixed and discretionary trusts. Evidential difficulty in locating particular members of a conceptually clear class is treated more leniently.

Key Points

  • Fixed trusts – complete list test.
  • Discretionary trusts – any given postulant test (McPhail v Doulton).
  • Conceptual uncertainty is fatal; evidential difficulty is treated more leniently.
