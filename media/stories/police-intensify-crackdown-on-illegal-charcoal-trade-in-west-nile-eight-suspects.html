The suspects face charges of illegal dealing in forest produce, in direct contravention of the law and a presidential directive

This latest enforcement action underscores a sustained government campaign to combat environmental degradation in Northern Uganda, where unchecked charcoal production and trade continue to threaten forest ecosystems, livelihoods, and long-term climate resilience.

Presidential Directive and Legal Backdrop

In May 2023, President Yoweri Kaguta Museveni issued Executive Order No. 3 of 2023on environmental protection. The order explicitly bans the burning, production, transportation, and commercial trade of charcoal across Northern and North Eastern Uganda, including the sub-regions of Karamoja, Teso, Lango, Acholi, and West Nile. The measure aims to curb rampant deforestation driven by commercial charcoal activities.

Police have warned that such activities are criminal and will be met with firm prosecution. Joint agencies vow to maintain operations, impound exhibits, and encourage public reporting of illegal forest exploitation.

Similar operations in recent months have netted dozens more suspects and thousands of bags of charcoal across Northern Uganda, signaling a broader push against environmental offenders.

The Environmental Crisis Driving the Crackdown

Charcoal production in Uganda, particularly in woodland and savanna regions like West Nile, has long been linked to significant deforestation. Trees are felled, carbonized in traditional earth kilns (often inefficient and smoky), and sold as a primary cooking fuel for urban and rural households. While charcoal provides energy and income for many, the scale of unregulated commercial production accelerates forest loss, soil erosion, biodiversity decline, and contributes to climate change through carbon emissions.

In Northern Uganda, where vegetation recovery can be slower due to climatic conditions and human pressures (including refugee settlements and agricultural expansion), experts have raised alarms about desertification risks. Streams drying up, reduced wildlife habitats, and declining agricultural productivity are among the reported consequences. The government views the ban in designated areas as a necessary intervention to allow forest regeneration while promoting alternatives like efficient cookstoves, briquettes, LPG, electricity, and sustainable woodlots.
